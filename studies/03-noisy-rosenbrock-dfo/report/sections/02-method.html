The Method

SPSA (Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation, Spall 1992) is the unfamiliar method here, so it got the full method-card treatment before any run. The idea in one breath: a finite-difference gradient in d dimensions costs 2d evaluations, but if you nudge all coordinates at once with a random ±1 vector Δ and take just two measurements, the quantity

is a wrong gradient whose wrongness has mean zero — so gradient descent on it still walks downhill, at two evaluations per step regardless of dimension. The catch is the gain sequences and : SPSA's guarantees are asymptotic, and its first steps are only as sane as is matched to your gradient magnitudes. Spall's 1998 implementation paper says exactly how to size it — from the desired magnitude of the first step. Remember that sentence; the journey section shows what happens to a researcher who reads it and then doesn't apply it.

The baselines: Nelder-Mead maintains a simplex of 3 points (in 2-D), reflecting/expanding/contracting by comparing function values — comparisons that noise can silently invert. Random restarts split the same 200 evaluations across k independent starts and keep the best finisher: exploration purchased at the price of per-start depth.

Findings and insights

The ladder closed the distance to the development ceiling of 0.884116 from 10.4555 measured floors at the raw-feature anchor to 1.7077 at the boosted incumbent, and the sealed partition returned 0.880949 against its own ceiling of 0.893469. The result a practitioner should take away is the second confirmed claim, and it carries the scope tag in-silico because it is a statement about behaviour under this declared process and not about the world: a boosted tree shown none of the true terms out-earned handing a linear model the true interaction — 4.7779 floors against 3.9699, and 4.6190 on the sealed rows. Nobody had to know which term to specify.

Where the linear model actually goes wrong

This is the plot only a known-truth study can draw: predicted probability against the true probability of the same rows, not against observed frequencies. And the shape of the error matters more than its size. The linear model's error is not noise but a clean sign flip — it over-predicts the low-probability rows and under-predicts the high-probability ones, crossing zero around the middle of the range. That is shrinkage toward the base rate, which is exactly what a model that cannot express the curvature of the true log-odds must do: unable to reach the extremes, it pulls every row toward the average.

The ranking gap and the probability gap are the same missing structure seen twice: Brier 0.132010 for the linear model against the boosted tree's 0.109337 and the irreducible Bayes floor of 0.103604.

The prediction the study got wrong, and the one it was wrong about

Five predictions were registered before any evidence existed. Four were supported and one refuted, every one adjudicated by arithmetic on a printed block rather than by prose. The family size is five, each bound to exactly one run, with no post-hoc selection — so the guard here is pre-registration rather than an alpha correction.

The second research question was refuted, and that is the most useful sentence on this page. It predicted the headroom would close before the fourth candidate was spent; it stood at 1.708 instead. The law did not stop the study, the study paid for the discard in full, and the prior that said otherwise was recorded as a miss rather than quietly rewritten.

Two replications that failed, and were kept

Both attempts to replicate the incumbent run failed, and neither failure was scientific. The first timed out: a replication builds its own environment in a detached worktree before the entrypoint runs at all, and this study's honest per-run budget was sized for runs that take under a second. The second crashed on a prepared artifact that the entrypoint reads but the contract never declared — so the replication harness, which copies exactly what the contract names, never copied it.

Both records stand at not reproduced. Neither was retried into a pass, and the entrypoint was not quietly edited afterwards to make an unproven fix look proven. The failure became a claim instead, which is the single most useful thing here for the next author.

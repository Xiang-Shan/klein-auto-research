Coding Advice

  1. Audit the headroom before you spend a challenger budget. One measured table — each rung's mean incumbent error against that rung's own floor — told this study, before a single challenger ran, that no training size could pay a keep. The 21-family parade then cost minutes and produced landing points instead of false hope. Compute how much room exists above your incumbent in floor units. If it is below one, re-scope the question; do not run the tournament and then discover the prize was never there.

  2. Pre-script the branch you are sure will not fire. The door-ajar branch carried roughly one-in-five odds and it fired. Because both branches existed as committed sentences, the surprise cost zero improvisation — and, more importantly, removed the temptation to redraw the split and try again. The contract that forbids the redraw is what makes the result worth reading.

  3. Call a guard a guard. The multiplicity procedure here is a randomization diagnostic under an assumption that the design does not supply. Naming it honestly cost one sentence; it also predicted its own weakness, because the fold-level sensitivity exhibit then failed to confirm the single detection. Publish the sensitivity exhibit beside the guard every time. If the verdict changes when the analysis unit changes, the reader deserves to know before you do.

  4. Your split law applies inside the estimators. A group-aware outer split proves nothing if the calibration wrapper, the hyper-parameter tuner and the stacking meta-learner all resplit rows internally. Pass precomputed group-aware splits into every one of them, and prefer external calibration over any estimator whose internal calibration you cannot control — the library itself is moving that way.

  5. Pass split policies, not split indices, into anything that refits. Absolute index lists are correct exactly once, on the data they were computed from; a stacking meta-learner refitting on row subsets will reject them, loudly if you are lucky. That crash cost one ledger row and taught the rule.

  6. Keep the crash rows. One hundred sixty arena crashes and three parade crashes are data, not mess. They located the Gaussian process's failure mode — separability, not scarcity — and they kept the guard family honest, because cells with no data occupy their slots as never-firing placeholders instead of quietly shrinking the multiplicity correction.

  7. Register seeds inside the numeric domain of the library that consumes them. This is study 07's lesson, reproduced verbatim by its own successor: twenty crashed trials on a bound that a comment in the previous study already warned about. Reading a lesson is not obeying it. Put the check in code, or you will pay for it twice.

The pattern under all seven: the discipline is portable even where the findings are not. Nothing here transfers to another dataset. The habit of measuring the bar before the contest, of writing both endings in advance, and of keeping the failures where a reviewer can find them — that transfers to every study you will run.

1 · The question

Put as many points as you can on the grid of lattice points so that no straight line passes through three of them — any line, not just rows, columns and diagonals. Dudeney posed it in 1900 with sixteen pawns on a chessboard.

The ceiling is free. Three points in one row are collinear, so each row holds at most two, and there are rows:

That is a one-line argument, and it is a theorem: no configuration found by any method can exceed it. The floor is not free — whether is actually attained at a given is a hard question, and past the small cases the answers come from computer search.

So this study asks a question with two halves, and the second half is the one it exists for:

On an integer grid, how many points can a budgeted iterated local search place with no three collinear — judged only by a declared exact verifier that shares no code with the search — at an where the proven maximum should be reachable and at an where it should not?

Why an optimize study, and why this problem

A search that scores itself is a witness testifying about its own case. Klein's optimize contract breaks that mechanically: the track declares a verifier, the verifier lives outside the per-experiment mutable surface, the METHOD gate hashes it so it can never change again, and klein run-one runs it as a second process on the artifact the search wrote. Its number is the one that enters the ledger. The searcher's own claim is recorded beside it, and a disagreement is a crash.

No-three-in-line is the right problem to demonstrate that on, for one reason: the objective is an integer — how many points are in the set — so the checker needs no tolerance. Two integers either agree or they do not.

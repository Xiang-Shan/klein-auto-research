The Method

The whole method fits in one sentence, and the sentence is worth memorizing before any of the algebra:

gQLS is a Q-Q plot fitted by generalized least squares.

You already do the first half by hand. Take your losses, plot the empirical quantiles against the theoretical quantiles of a candidate law, and eyeball whether the points fall on a line. If they do, the intercept of that line is your location parameter and the slope is your scale parameter. Quantile least squares (QLS) stops eyeballing and runs the regression. The g — generalized — runs it with the right error covariance instead of pretending the plotted points are equally reliable and mutually independent.

Why a small set of quantiles buys robustness

Point maximum likelihood at the 30 hurricane losses and every observation gets a vote, weighted by the score function — including the 1926 Great Miami storm at $72.303bn, which is 2.185× the second-largest loss in the sample and 20.5% of the total damage mass on its own. The MLE has no mechanism for saying “that one is unusual.” Inflate that single number tenfold and the lognormal σ̂ moves from 0.833868 to 1.097494 — a +31.6% shift in the one parameter that governs the entire tail, from one corrupted record out of thirty.

QLS never looks at that observation. It reads the sample at k = 8 probability levels between a = 0.05 and b = 0.95 and fits the quantile curve through those eight points. The largest observation sits above p = 0.95; nothing in the estimating equations consults it. Corrupt it, delete it, multiply it by a million — the estimate does not move. That is not a heuristic. It is the definition of a breakdown point:

— the fraction of the sample you may corrupt arbitrarily before the estimate can be driven anywhere.

Read it as a count, because that is what makes it actionable. At (a, b) = (0.05, 0.95) the breakdown point is 5%, and 5% of 30 events is 1.5 — so one corrupted event is tolerated and two are not. At (0.10, 0.90) it is 10%, so three are tolerated. The trim you choose is the number of bad records you are buying protection against. There is no free lunch: the same trim that ignores the corrupted maximum also ignores the genuine maximum.

Why the “generalized” matters

Ordinary QLS (oQLS) runs plain OLS on that Q-Q plot. That silently assumes the eight plotted points are equally reliable and mutually independent. They are neither. Sample quantiles in the sparse tail wobble far more than sample quantiles near the median, and adjacent quantiles from the same sample are strongly positively correlated — they share order statistics.

The fix is generalized least squares: down-weight the noisy points and account for the correlation. GLS normally stalls right here, because you would have to estimate the error covariance from the data — and estimating a k×k covariance from 30 points is worse than the disease. In this problem you do not have to. Serfling's joint asymptotic normality theorem hands you the covariance of the empirical quantile vector in closed form, and after standardization it depends only on the standard member of the family — no unknown parameters at all. You can write down before seeing a single data point.

How much does that buy? The thesis's asymptotic relative efficiencies (k = 25, (0.05, 0.95)) answer bluntly:

For a heavy standard member, ordinary QLS throws away roughly 77% of the available information and gQLS throws away almost none. That is the single largest effect in this method. And it is directly visible in the data being reproduced: on the hurricane sample at (0.05, 0.95), log-Cauchy's σ̂ is 0.229912 under oQLS and 0.485055 under gQLS — a factor of 2.11 from the weighting alone. That split is this study's specification falsifier: if a from-scratch is wrong, those two numbers will not separate that way. Section 4 shows it passing at a deviation of 8.8e−05.

Read the Normal row too, because it is the honest counterweight: gQLS is very slightly worse than oQLS there. For a light, well-behaved member the identity-matrix pretence costs almost nothing, and the GLS weighting's finite-sample overhead is not repaid. The gain is concentrated exactly where the tail is heavy — which is where loss data lives.

(2.4) / (2.5) — why it is robust, formally. The influence function of each sample quantile is a bounded step function, and both estimators are fixed linear maps applied to that bounded vector. Boundedness is what “one bad record cannot move it far” means; positivity of BP is what “several bad records still cannot” means. They are different guarantees, and this study tests both — contamination for the first (RQ3), deletion for the second (RQ4). Section 5 shows the distinction turning out to matter far more than expected.

So the method's headline claim (“robust parameters”) and the decision-maker's actual question (“stable 1-in-100”) can come apart — and they may come apart worst for the best-fitting model. That is RQ5 and RQ6, and it was genuinely open when the loop began.

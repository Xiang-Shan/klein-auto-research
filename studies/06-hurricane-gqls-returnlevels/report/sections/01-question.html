The Question

A catastrophe pricing actuary has thirty numbers: the thirty most damaging hurricanes to hit the United States between 1900 and 1995, each normalized to 1995 dollars for inflation, wealth and coastal population. From those thirty numbers a committee wants one number — the 1-in-100 event loss — and that number will set a catastrophe layer, a capital charge, or a solvency return.

Now suppose one of the thirty rows is wrong. Not missing, not obviously absurd — just wrong by a factor of ten, the way a decimal-point slip or a double-counted recovery makes a number wrong. No data-quality process catches it, because $723bn for the 1926 Great Miami hurricane is only implausible if you already know the answer.

How much does the 1-in-100 move? And is there an estimator that would not have moved at all?

A 2024 doctoral thesis says yes and shows the parameters standing still. It never carries those parameters through to a return level. That gap — between a robust parameter and a stable decision — is what this study exists to measure. The registered goal:

On the 30 most-damaging US hurricanes (Pielke-Landsea 1998, normalized 1995 USD), does a from-scratch gQLS reproduce Adjieteh (2024) Table 6.9 within the published reporting resolution; does its robustness advantage over MLE survive the thesis's 10× contamination and leave-top-k-out stress; and does that advantage carry into per-event return levels — the thesis's own declared future work?

Two tracks, because there are two different objects here

The goal contains two questions that a single leaderboard would silently blend. Klein answers that with tracks: independent metric frontiers that are never merged, each owning its own direction, its own measured minimum_delta, and its own guardrails.

TrackMetricWhat it measuresminimum_deltaGuardrails
reproduction mean_abs_param_deviation (lower) Distance-to-published. Mean |Δθ| over the 36 published Table-6.9 parameters — a property of our implementation, not of the data. 0.005 — half the last published digit max |Δθ| ≤ 0.02 · max |ΔW| ≤ 0.10 · max |Δp| ≤ 0.02 · wall_seconds ≤ 120
decision return_level_instability_pct (lower) Decision stability. Max |% change| of the fitted 1-in-100 event loss across the adaptive stress set — the actuarial decision unit. 1.0 pp — an ordering device, see below w_pvalue ≥ 0.10 · wall_seconds ≤ 120

Two design choices in that table are load-bearing and worth stating explicitly.

Why the reproduction metric is not a goodness-of-fit statistic. The obvious metric for "did we implement this right" is something like a W statistic or an AIC. It would be the wrong object. A GoF statistic measures whether the model fits the data; this track asks whether our code reproduces their printed table. Those are different questions with different failure modes, and only the second one can be answered against 156 numbers that were fixed in print before this study existed. The mean over 36 parameters keeps the frontier well-behaved as specification bugs get fixed; the max guardrail exists so that one broken cell cannot hide behind a good average. Section 4 shows the max guardrail earning its keep twice — once on a real bug, once on something that turned out not to be ours at all.

The w_pvalue guardrail is an anti-degeneracy device. On a stability metric, the trivially optimal model is one that predicts nothing: an estimator whose 1-in-100 never moves because it never responds to anything would score 0.0 and win. Requiring the in-sample goodness-of-fit test to PASS (W p ≥ 0.10) turns the question into the one an actuary actually asks: among models that FIT, which gives the most stable 1-in-100? Without it, the decision track would have been a race to the most useless model.

Six research questions, each with a prior registered before the loop

Every prior below was written into study.yaml before any experiment ran, so every one of them could come out wrong. Three did — including the one the study was built to ask.

RQ1Does a from-scratch gQLS reproduce all 36 parameters of Table 6.9 within the 0.005 reporting resolution?Yes — the specification is complete once eq. 3.8 and the standard-member table are used (pre-verified at the method gate)

RQ5Among configurations that PASS the in-sample GoF test, which estimator × family gives the most stable 1-in-100 event loss?Genuinely uncertain. log-Cauchy fits best but its quantile transform amplifies σ̂ by tan(0.49π) = 31.8 at p = 0.99. The best-fitting model may be the least decision-stable — the study's sharpest open question

RQ1–RQ3 are reproduction: they ask whether the published work can be rebuilt from its own description. RQ4–RQ6 are extension: no published work carries these fits through to a 1-in-100 event loss, and none runs a leave-top-k-out stress on this sample. The thesis's own §7.2 names risk measures as future work, so the second half of the study is genuinely new rather than a re-derivation.

The sealed design — and the honesty it demands

Klein seals a final-test partition and allows each track exactly one access to it. With n = 30 there is no partition to hold out; you cannot spare six hurricanes. So this study seals something else: published third-party numbers. Adaptive work was contractually confined to Table 6.9 (36 parameters). The sealed reproduction target was the full Table 6.10 grid — 5 estimator arms × 6 families × original and 10×-modified data = 60 cells, 120 parameters the loop never touched. The sealed decision target was the thesis's exact modification (72.303 → 723.03); adaptive stress was capped at 5×.

That is a genuinely strong anti-peeking guarantee in one direction. The targets are third-party numbers fixed in print before the study existed: they cannot be moved by anything the study does, and unlike a random fold, an analyst cannot in principle inspect them ahead of time without breaking the contract. But the design is structurally blind in another direction, and the data card wrote the constraint before any modeling began. It is carried here verbatim because it is exactly the sentence that erodes first in a slide deck:

Standing rule (e). “Sealed evidence = independent PUBLISHED TARGETS, not independent data — confirmation wording is ‘implementation fidelity + within-sample robustness’, never out-of-sample generalization.” The same 30 observations underlie both the adaptive and the sealed evaluation, so sampling error is fully common-mode and the sealed test is structurally blind to it.

Read every confirmed label in section 5 through that rule. It means our implementation reproduces their published numbers on their sample, and the estimator is robust within that sample. It never means the next hurricane sample will behave the same way.

One further consequence, registered up front rather than discovered later: the decision track's minimum_delta of 1.0 pp is an ordering device on a fixed 30-event sample, not a confidence statement. At n = 30 a parametric bootstrap puts σ̂ = 0.8339 inside [0.617, 1.034] — a ±25% relative band on the single parameter that drives the entire tail. Every instability percentage in this report ranks estimators on these thirty events under this stress. Section 2 explains why that delta was kept at 1.0 rather than quietly shrunk to manufacture significance.

Training-recipe advice

The change. Replace a constant learning rate with a cosine decay to a tenth of the peak over the whole budget. The matched-compute condition it held under. 2000 AdamW steps, the same 4-layer 4-head 128-wide causal transformer (824320 parameters), on the same 891904 training characters — every candidate at exactly the same step count, enforced by the checker reading steps out of the checkpoint rather than promised by the training script. The seed spread it was measured against. A paired-comparison bar of 0.0149525 nats. The size of the change. 0.052855 nats, 3.53486 floors — it clears the bar by more than three times, and it is the only one of six single changes that clears it at all. The hardware. Apple silicon, MPS-selected by default; the same checkpoint re-scores identically on CPU. What this does not say. It does not say cosine decay beats a schedule this study never ran, and it does not say the improvement survives to unseen text: the sealed block was 3.140679 floors harder than development for the same recipe, and with one track and one sealed access there is no sealed measurement of the anchor to difference against. Nothing here is priced — this study registers no materiality block, so “improvement” means only that a pre-registered, measured bar was cleared.

Six lessons, each with the run that paid for it

  1. Measure your floor on the quantity your decision actually uses. The five-seed LEVEL spread here has standard deviation 0.00802952; the ten paired DIFFERENCES between two seeds of the same recipe have standard deviation 0.00747623 and range 0.026494, and it is the paired spread a candidate-versus-anchor comparison has to clear. A level spread and a comparison spread are different quantities, and the gap between them is exactly why this contract derives minimum_delta from the second one, never the first.

  2. At a fixed step budget, spend the schedule before you spend the capacity. The only keep in this study was a pure schedule change worth 0.052855 nats, while multiplying the parameter count by 3.908075 was worth -0.4742 floors. A step budget rewards changing WHEN the optimizer moves, not how much it has to move with.

  3. Never let the training script grade its own checkpoint. It costs one extra file: a checker that loads the artifact, asks the contract which rows are the validation set, rebuilds the model from the checkpoint’s own config with its own implementation, and recomputes the loss. Here the two never disagreed by a printable amount against a declared tolerance of 0.01 — the outcome you want, and one you cannot assume without checking.

  4. Put a copy-the-input control in your language-model harness. It bets the next character equals the current one, so a correct harness scores it WORSE than chance — 4.750043 against 4.174388 nats here — while the classic off-by-one target-alignment bug would score it 0.693147. None of this study’s other three controls could have caught that particular bug.

  5. If you split ordered text into contiguous blocks, measure the distribution shift you just bought instead of assuming it away. The identical recipe with MORE training data scored 0.046961 nats worse on the sealed block than on the development block. A contiguous split buys leakage safety; it does not buy identically-distributed partitions, and the honest move is to measure the difference rather than hope it is zero.

  6. Decide at design time how a comparison gets its sealed number. One track and one sealed access can confirm a LEVEL and never a DIFFERENCE. This study’s contract declared one track and said nothing about the gap, so its headline schedule improvement stays exploratory by arithmetic, not by modesty. Either give each family its own track, or pre-register the gap itself as exploratory-by-construction — deciding afterwards is how sealed vocabulary gets stretched.

The entrypoint that produced every number above

The mutable surface this study’s search actually touches is exactly two files. model.py carries the architecture and the CONFIG dict a width or weight-tying candidate edits; verify.py — outside the mutable surface, hashed at the METHOD gate — carries its OWN separate implementation of the same class, so a candidate that changes the module tree produces a checkpoint the checker cannot load, and the run crashes rather than silently comparing two different architectures.

Three details in that file are worth reading deliberately rather than skimming
past. The batching is a streamed index shuffle straight out of a numpy array
— no DataLoader, no TensorDataset — because on
Apple-silicon MPS that combination has silently collapsed predictions to a constant in
earlier work in this repository; drawing offsets directly from the token array sidesteps
the failure mode entirely rather than working around it after the fact. The
model is built on CPU and moved to the device afterward
(arch.build(config).to(device)), which is deliberate and easy to delete
by accident: it is what makes a CPU run and an MPS run of the same seed start from
identical weights, and is the reason the device-agreement check in the journey section
above is a meaningful comparison rather than a coincidence. The partition
never comes from a literal seed or a hardcoded split — every run asks
kleinlib.data.load_partition, which prints the realized
split_fingerprint back for the notary to compare against the one frozen
at the DATA gate. A study elsewhere in this repository once hardcoded a retired split
seed directly into an evaluator and silently scored the wrong partition for an entire
ledger lane; the fingerprint check exists specifically so that failure mode is caught
by the notary before a single number is trusted, not discovered later by a numeral
that happens to look wrong.

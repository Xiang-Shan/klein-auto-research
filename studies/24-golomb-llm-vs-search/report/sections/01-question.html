What you will take away

  • At a matched number of verifier calls, the LLM arm matched the tabulated length on more development pairs than the classical search did (6 of 12 against 0 of 12), and on the sealed orders neither arm matched (0 of 6 each). The development contrast is a count difference on two orders, not an established rate (claims C2, C3).
  • The recall signature that was registered (a match already in the first proposal batch) was not seen: 1 of 12 pairs, with the other matches arriving after verifier feedback (claim C4). That refutes the registered rule; it does not exclude recall.
  • Every verdict is the verifier's number, not the searcher's, and every matched ruler re-attains a length that OEIS A003022 already lists. The study establishes nothing new about any Golomb ruler (claim C16).

The decision this study informs

Claims that a language model “found an optimal combinatorial object” are now common. A reader weighing one needs two things the headline rarely gives: who checked the object, and whether the model searched or remembered. This study builds the smallest honest test of both. The object is the Golomb ruler: integer marks on a ruler such that every pairwise distance occurs once. The shortest ruler for each is tabulated in OEIS A003022 and in public tables, so the answer key is printed in the textbook.

The question, as registered in study.yaml:

At a matched budget of verifier calls, does an LLM proposing Golomb rulers as data match the known tabulated lengths as often as a classical randomized search, on development orders 8–11 and sealed orders 12–13, and is a match search or recall?

The decision the answer feeds is practical: should a claimed LLM match on a combinatorial benchmark be read as search, as recall, or as neither until a control says otherwise?

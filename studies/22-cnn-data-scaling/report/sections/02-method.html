The method taught

A fitted exponent is a tangent line, not a law

On log-log axes almost any smooth, decreasing curve looks straight over a short enough interval. A fit over 1.5 decades measures the average local slope over that interval. That slope can be pinned precisely and still be the wrong slope to extrapolate with. The question the study asks is not “how well is the line pinned?” but “is the curve still a line out there?”

The law and how it is fitted

The pure law is a power in the training-set size, and taking logs turns the fit into ordinary least squares on the six seed-mean losses:

Each cell is a 3-seed array and the fit uses the seed mean of the development cross-entropy at each of the six sizes. R² is computed in log space. The printed interval for is a percentile bootstrap that resamples seeds within each N. Because subsamples are nested within a seed (the 500 images are part of the 1,000, and so on), that bootstrap is only a descriptive spread, never a calibrated 95% interval.

Why the curve might bend

For a fixed model the data curve is expected to saturate at a model-dependent floor. The saturating alternative is

A floor makes the local slope decay toward zero as N grows, so a straight line fitted on the steep early part predicts a loss that is too low further out: the pure law would be optimistic. That is the bet behind P4 and P6. R² cannot arbitrate: the method card's synthetic checks show a curve whose pure-law extrapolation misses by 20 percent can still score R² above 0.98.

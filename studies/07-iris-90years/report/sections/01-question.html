The Question

In 1936, R. A. Fisher printed 150 iris flowers — four measurements each — and a closed-form rule for telling species apart. Two of his three species, versicolor and virginica, overlap: this is the hard pair, and Fisher said so in print — "there is some overlap of the distributions of I. virginica and I. versicolor, so that a certain diagnosis of these two species could not be based solely on these four measurements of a single flower taken on a plant growing wild." Ninety years later this study asks the question he declined: on his own hard pair, judged honestly, does anything beat Fisher's 1936 rule?

The study's motto, written before any run: 「让 AI 通宵做研究不难；难的是天亮之后，你敢在结论上签字。」 — "Overnight AI research is easy. The hard part is a conclusion you dare to sign the next morning."

The contenders are a 90-year ladder of method history, all pre-registered: logistic regression (Berkson 1944 / Cox 1958), k-nearest neighbours (Fix–Hodges 1951 / Cover–Hart 1967), SVM-RBF (Cortes–Vapnik 1995), and histogram gradient boosting (Friedman 2001 / sklearn 2019) — plus two LDA ablations (petal-only, sepal-only) and one rung that is designed to crash.

The real subject is the discipline that makes the answer signable: three gates before any run, a scouting ledger that discloses every number the design was shaped by, a measured floor (δ = 0.033) instead of a guessed significance bar, an append-only 9-transaction ledger where discards and crashes stay as evidence, and exactly one sealed look at 20 rows nothing was ever tuned on. On 100 flowers and a 20-row development partition, the question "which method is better" turns out to be mostly a question about which split you drew — and the protocol is what keeps that fact from choosing the winner.
